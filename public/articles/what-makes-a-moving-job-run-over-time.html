What Makes a Moving Job Run Over Time — And How Customers Can Avoid It

When you book a removal service, you're booking a time slot — not an open-ended window. Here's how to keep yours on track.

When you book a removal service, you're booking a time slot — not an open-ended window. But anyone in the moving industry knows that jobs regularly run past their allotted time, and it's rarely the moving team's fault. More often than not, small oversights on the customer's side add minutes that stack into hours by the end of the job.

The good news is that almost every cause of extra time is preventable. Here's a breakdown of the most common behaviors that push a move past its booked slot, and what customers can do differently to keep things running on schedule.

1. Not Being Packed and Ready Before the Team Arrives

The single biggest time-waster in any move is a customer who isn't actually ready when the movers show up. If boxes aren't packed, taped, and stacked by the time the team arrives, the movers end up waiting around — or worse, helping to finish packing before they can even start loading.

A moving team's time is calculated from the moment they're expected to begin moving items, not from the moment they start helping you finish packing. Every box that gets sealed after arrival is time taken directly out of the booked window.

What to do instead: Have every box packed, taped shut, and labelled before the scheduled arrival time. Furniture that needs to be disassembled should also be sorted out in advance where possible. The goal is simple: when the team walks in, there should be nothing left to do but carry things out.

2. Obstructions Along the Moving Path

Movers need a clear, unobstructed route from where the items are to the van. Furniture in hallways, rugs bunched up near doorways, pets wandering underfoot, or narrow gaps cluttered with shoes and bags all slow down every single trip back and forth.

It might not seem like much on any one trip, but a move typically involves dozens of trips between the property and the van. If each trip takes an extra 10–15 seconds because someone has to step around a chair or push a door out of the way, that adds up to real, measurable time by the end of the job.

What to do instead: Before the team arrives, walk the exact path they'll be using — from each room, down the hallway or stairs, out the door, to the van — and clear anything that isn't part of the move. Secure pets in a separate room. If you're in a building with lifts or shared corridors, make sure those are also accessible and not blocked by other tenants' items.

3. Boxes Not Grouped by Size or Type

Efficient loading depends on organisation. When boxes of similar sizes are grouped together, movers can load the van in a way that maximises space and minimises the number of trips needed. When boxes are scattered — a large box here, a small one there, mixed in with loose items — the team has to search for what fits together, slowing down both the carrying and the loading process.

This also affects how items are protected in transit. Similarly sized and weighted boxes stack more securely, reducing the risk of shifting or damage on the road, which in turn avoids the delays that come with re-packing a van mid-route.

What to do instead: Group boxes by size in the same area of the room — small boxes together, medium boxes together, large ones together — and keep fragile items clearly marked and separated. If possible, place them in the order you'd like them loaded, closest to the door first.

4. Not Offering to Help With Lighter Items

Customers are never expected to lift heavy furniture or awkward items — that's what the professional team is there for. But there's a difference between staying out of the way and actively helping the process move faster.

Carrying light items — small boxes, lamps, cushions, bags — alongside the moving team can meaningfully cut down the total number of trips required, especially in larger properties or ones with long walks to the van. It's a small contribution, but it adds up over the course of a job.

What to do instead: Ask the team what you can help carry. Light, non-fragile, easy-to-grab items are the safest place to pitch in. This isn't about doing the movers' job for them — it's about keeping the process efficient on both sides.

5. Not Confirming the ETA to the Drop-Off Point

Once everything is loaded onto the van, there's often a gap between departure and arrival at the new address — and that gap needs to be actively managed, not assumed. If the customer doesn't request an estimated time of arrival before the van departs, it becomes harder to plan for what happens at the other end: access arrangements, parking, lift bookings, or simply being present to receive the delivery.

Delays at the drop-off point — waiting for someone to arrive, waiting for access to be sorted, waiting for a parking spot to free up — eat into time just as much as delays at pickup.

What to do instead: Before the van leaves the pickup address, ask the team for their expected arrival time at the destination. Use that window to make sure someone is on-site, ready, and that access (parking, lift, building permissions) is already sorted rather than being figured out on arrival.

6. Booking or Moving During Peak Traffic Hours

Traffic is one factor customers can't control once a van is on the road — but it is a factor they can plan around when booking. In London, for example, peak periods tend to fall between 10am and 1pm, and journeys crossing the city from east to west during this window are especially prone to delays. A move that would normally take 30 minutes on the road can stretch well past an hour once traffic is added into the mix.

Because time on the road is still time counted against the job, a move scheduled through peak traffic is more likely to run over than one scheduled around it — even when everything else about the move is handled perfectly.

What to do instead: Where there's flexibility, avoid booking moves that require significant travel through peak hours, particularly cross-city journeys. If a peak-time slot is unavoidable, build in extra time when planning your day so a longer-than-expected journey doesn't throw off other commitments.

The Bigger Picture

None of these behaviors are about blame — they're about awareness. A booked time slot reflects an estimate based on a smooth, well-prepared move. Every obstruction, every unpacked box, every unclear ETA, and every avoidable stretch of peak-hour traffic adds friction that pushes the job past that estimate.

The customers who get the most out of their booking are the ones who treat preparation as part of the job, not something separate from it. Pack ahead of time, clear the path, organise by size, lend a hand where it's safe to, stay informed about timing on both ends, and plan around traffic where possible. Do these six things, and there's a very good chance your move finishes exactly when it was supposed to — on time, and without any surprises.
